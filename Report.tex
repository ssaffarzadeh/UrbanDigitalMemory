\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

The urban environments have always been transforming through different
eras. The transformations caused by designers and planners have
undergone a metamorphosis, starting from blueprints to co-design with
citizens (Arnstein 2019; Horelli and Wallin 2024; Mueller et al. 2018).
In the past few years, the social justice approaches amplified the
concentration on engaging the public in urban interventions. However,
not all countries have a democratic structure to do so.

In countries like Iran, despite following the theories and works of
citizen engagement in academia, the practice is far behind this mindset.
Many urban development projects are implemented with a top-down
approach, regardless of the responsible party. Whether a contractor,
developer, designer, planner, or policy maker, none ever approached the
public for their opinions on an urban project. There are minimal
practices that tried to include citizens within their work by running
surveys or interviews. Nevertheless, the number of participators have
barely met the satisfaction of the affected population, which looked
more like being manipulated (Arnstein 2019). The planning and design
approaches in countries with less democratic political systems would
have the least amount of successful public engagement through their
process.

Although active participation has never been an easy practice, there
have been tools and platforms that made the process smoother for both
ends. Living in the digital era, many citizens have access to online
social platforms where they can usually speak up on their own, with the
chance that their opinion could be heard nationally and even globally.
In such an era, Iran is not an exception either. Although Iranians have
filtered internet access, they use VPNs to bypass the restrictions and
access the free internet, sharing their opinions, voices, cries,
compliments, memories, and all other experiences unaccessible on online
social networks.

This new accessibility can be considered as a shortcut to democracy.
Sharing the news and posts regarding urban spaces is an ongoing
phenomenon that is more than just a shared media with a caption. It can
be seen as acknowledging a new space, or the opposite, downgrading a new
intervention in the city. The background of a city can now be traced
within the online platforms run by individuals who captured it
throughout time. This doesn't end with the past of urban spaces, but
also how an urban space changes in real-time, which would have its own
responses. How citizens react to an urban project is evident on social
media, and how they perceive the transformed urban space as before. The
urban space is not just a physical or mental entity; it's also digital.

This study aims to tackle the matter of how citizens' lived experiences
shared on social media form as urban digital memories. The digital
memory creates newer concepts on top of the right to the city notion
that can be called right to the memory. The project focuses on how the
right to the memory can become a significant tool to validate urban
interventions on spatial features through a digital public engagement
and participation perspective.

\section{Problem Statement}\label{problem-statement}

There are urban spaces, specifically, public spaces that hold a large
pool of collective and individual memories within them, either tragic,
joyful, or revolutionary (Solnit 2009). City Theater's Square\footnote{Teatré
  Shahr in Persian {[}translated by the author{]}} is not an exception.
However, the public apparently doesn't have any right or authority over
the space and the memories within it as common property. Some spaces get
manipulated, transformed, or even demolished by government enforced by
economic or political interests(Paccoud 2016). The example of it would
be the Haussmannian boulevards that demolished the existing urban
streets into wide and long boulevards with luxury retail shops on the
sides and policing to oppress any opposition from citizens in the most
dominance possible (Harvey 2006). The citizens don't have any agency
over the space to make decisions, or at least to be informed clearly
about the interventions or changes made by the actors on the higher
ground. Not only with the actors, but also with the decision makers,
this applies too. Right to the memory is a metaphor of
collective/individual rights over space tied to memories that emerge
within it. This represents a bond made between the space and its
dwellings and visitors by memories, emotions, presence, and activities
that shaped each person's lived-experience in that space. Nevertheless,
this connection is taken for granted by the actors, designers, policy
makers and governments holding the upper hand of power. Sometimes it's
even ignored by them or does conflict with their main interests.

The memory is a valuable lived-experience that holds evidence of a
lived, conceived and perceived physical space for each individual (Shaw
and Graham 2017).

shared with the public. The design proposal depicted a completely new
spatial layout for the plaza. And, as part of the reconstruction, a part
of the central space was demolished (Fig.2). As soon as the public found
out about the intervention, a Twitter bombardment happened, consisting
of dissatisfied or even angry opinions mentioning how precious the
fountain and the plaza were to them, and that was easily transformed

\section{Research Objectives}\label{research-objectives}

In the era in which digital online media holds a vast impact on urban
lives, it ends up even becoming an autonomous memorial archive (Haskins
2007). Having such a large-scale individual lived-experience from
citizens would help to gain a better understanding of citizens' reviews
towards urban environments. Also, it's not only about the opinions, but
also about how they experienced the space individually or collectively.
This is a helpful step towards planning and designing projects.

Also, by the right to the city notions (Harvey 2003; Lefebvre 1968), it
was discussed that memories of a space that holds dozens of
lived-experiences from various individuals or groups, turn the space
into a valuable environment, a place (Martin 2025). Having a place is
not limited to its physical existence, but also how it's cloned in the
memories of those who had interactions with it. This would be the link
that having the right to the city will be followed up by having the
right to the memory.

Having an already-expressed opinion or archived memories on open and
accessible platforms would aid the designers and planners to study a
citizen perspective and eventually find more efficient strategies
towards public engagement (Williamson and Ruming 2020). The digital
memory may not be the final solution, but it will be a key starting
point for many urban transformations aiming to be more inclusive and
plural.

The objectives can be defined as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Public engagement increase via virtual spaces (online digital
  platforms)
\item
  Inclusive design and planning
\item
  Digitally shared lived-experience validation as digital memories
  (right to the memory)
\item
  Design / Planning through urban data
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Teatré Shahr (City
Theater)}\label{teatruxe9-shahr-city-theater}

One of the public squares which has always been under drastic tension
between its users and the ones who want to change it is the City
Theater\footnote{Teatré Shahr in Persian {[}Translated by the author{]}}
Square\footnote{The square holds the city theater building within its
  space, which could be considered a semi-open public space in Tehran,
  with all of the activities and events that take place downtown.}. As
can be seen in (Fig. 2), the square is in the intersection of Vali'asr
and Enghelab streets, which are the longest NS / EW armatures of Tehran.

\begin{figure}[H]

{\centering \pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{images/teatre shahr.jpg}}

}

\caption{\emph{Figure 1. Tehran's overview and City Theater's location
within it.\\
Source: Google Earth, 2024}}

\end{figure}%

\begin{figure}[H]

{\centering \pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{images/4811609.jpg}}

}

\caption{\emph{Figure 2. City Theater's building and plaza.\\
Source: Hamshahri Online, 2024}.}

\end{figure}%

The reason behind picking this specific public space is not only its
architectural quality as a public space, but its historical legacy,
which holds many urban events, activities, and daily-life patterns
within itself. A public space at the heart of the Iranian capital,
Tehran, has always been a focal point for its dwellers.

The City Theater Square holds one of the oldest theaters in Tehran,
dating back to the 1970s. The City Theater of Tehran building was built
as an art and cultural complex. City Theater was not only a venue, but
also a taste of the contemporary design from Iranian
architecture(wikipedia 2026; Mohit 2026). The presence of the building
with its surroundings formed a public space since the complex was ready
for productivity.

\begin{figure}[H]

{\centering \pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{images/City_Theater_of_Tehran_-_1971.jpg}}

}

\caption{\emph{Figure 3. City Theater Building of Tehran in 1971}}

\end{figure}%

As can be seen in Figs.2 \& 3, the building and the plaza surrounding it
have faced many transformations, such as the spatial composition,
accessibility, mobility, vegetation, and urban furniture. City Theater
Square has always been a central point of the daily urban life of
Tehran. Moreover, the square has been the ground of various social and
political events since its establishment. Thus, it holds a large scale
of digital memories within itself to collect and study. Also, due to a
lack of an explicit online platform for citizens to share their digital
memories regarding a specific urban space, the current study focuses on
the most popular and recognized public space. This will increase the
chance of related data availability and collection.

\subsection[X and Instagram]{\texorpdfstring{X and
Instagram\footnote{Since the mentioned social media platforms are
  officially banned in Iran, there aren't precise data or information
  regarding the user accounts in Iran. The current descriptions are
  based on the author's experiences who was born and raised in Iran
  using the mentioned social media platforms that are popular among the
  existing internet users who access them via VPNs that change their IP
  addresses in Iran.}}{X and Instagram}}\label{x-and-instagram4}

Multiple social media platforms hold much data regarding digital
memories. In the Iranian case, users are mostly active in the two
platforms of X (formerly Twitter) and Meta's Instagram. The X has always
been one of the most dynamic platforms for Iranians to share their
social, political, and daily-life opinions about the state of the
country or their living environments. Instagram is the most popular
social media platform among Iranians by a percentage of 28.33\%, and X
holds the percentage of 5.4 throughout the last decade (Dec 2015 - Dec
2025) (statcounter, n.d.). X may not be the most popular platform, but
it's the space in which many users share their opinions on critical or
general topics more straightforwardly and transparently. While other
platforms like Instagram, Facebook, or Pinterest are concentrated on
sharing media and general descriptions, X would bring more textual
content that holds more individual or professional positionality
regarding a topic.

Based on these reasons, X and Instagram are chosen as the targeted
social media platforms to collect their digital memories about the City
Theater Square and analyze the texts stored in tweets and Instagram
posts.

\section{Literature Review
(Relevance)}\label{literature-review-relevance}

\subsubsection{Right to the memory as a right to the
city}\label{right-to-the-memory-as-a-right-to-the-city}

The Right to the City was coined by the French philosopher, Henry
Lefebvre (1968), during the era in which the social movements became
viral among the young intellectuals and labor activists who'd been
seeking social justice within the urban areas (Mitchell 2003). After
Modernism, the ownership of the land in urban areas through a capitalist
monopoly kicked off dramatically. At that time, politicians, social
activists, urbanists, and philosophers realized the need for a just city
and a~healthy environment between the city blocks is something crucial
and, in other words, could be considered a right for the citizens who
live in there and use its space (Habibi and Amiri 2016; Harvey 2015;
Lefebvre 1968).

Furthermore, the notion was developed by Harvey (2015), who argued that
city dwellers can fight for their right over the space and generally,
over the city. This act works against the manipulation and trespassing
of the public domain by private developers and governments who attempt
to carry out capitalism all over the vacant spaces for profit. Harvey
(2015) suggested that this could be prevented by the public with social
activities and demonstrations by knowing the Right to the City as their
individual will to make a change in their living space and ultimately
change themselves. Thus, the Right to the City is mostly interpreted as
the right to public space. Who has access to it and who doesn't defines
this right (Harvey 2015; Habibi and Amiri 2016; Moroni and Chiodelli
2014).

If we consider the right over public space derived from the concept of
the right to the city. Once people have their lived-experiences on a
space, it forms their memories of that space. Since memory and physical
space can be imagined as intertwined existences, the memories are
attached to the physical space while the space holds the memories within
its boundaries as well (notions of sense of place, etc.). Thus, having
the right to a public space automatically gives the right to the
memories of it at the same time. In other words, policy makers,
designers, or any other entity that holds the authority or power to
change the space have to take into account the sensitivity of a public
space, not only based on general spatial values and qualities, but also
with memories embedded within that space. It should be acknowledged that
citizens have a right to the space, and they have the right to their
memories of that space as common goods, too.

Based on the same theoretical principles, the right to the city has
always been discussed by Harvey (1993) to exceed further than just
having access to the resources (Tedeschi 2024).

The abstract space described by Lefebvre (1991), limited to planning and
design perspectives, has changed into a rapid representation in various
forms through digital information. The space is no longer an abstraction
of a physical entity, but it is regenerated and transformed in a digital
domain based on its rapidly changing information (Shaw and Graham 2017).

The technological development has brought new definitions of the right
to the city concept. Tedeschi (2024) argues that algorithms have a right
over space, for they produce and update information regarding physical
space rapidly. This makes the accessibility and experience for its
dwellers either disruptive or reinforcing, which leads them to negotiate
their social intakes and power over space with such a non-human entity.
This creates a different level of inequality in a digital dimension.

inequalities of digital space

\subsubsection{Digital era (archival
memories)}\label{digital-era-archival-memories}

In the digital age, the media has become a tool for any individual to
express their thoughts, talk about their feelings, bring up ideas, share
experiences and memories, or cry out for their right as critical matter.
One major feature of digital media is their archival potential. They are
the database of memories of many individual and collective activities or
events in a variety of contexts.

What archival memories can bring is not comparable to a curated,
centrally organised museum set up exclusively for visitors as the
audience, but an independent multi-voice platform that anyone holding a
digital device can share and publish to other users throughout the
internet (Haskins 2007). Even such an idea was somehow suggested by
Lefebvre (2003) that having a self-management feature over a digital
platform should be a right, even though it was considered as a right to
urban resources and not something higher (Tedeschi 2024).

The importance of the digital domain is not just about having horizontal
archival memories made by numerous individuals, but the digital space
itself as a phenomenon that can trace the contents of a society
regarding a physical space. Shaw and Graham (2017, 909) brings up the
importance of data flows in urban studies:

\begin{quote}
``\emph{Just as the urbanization of water is a processional notion which
can uncover `stories about the city's structure and development', so can
a reading of the city's `political, social, and economic conduits'
through which information flows also ``carry the potential for an
improved, more just, and more equitable right to the city''}''
\end{quote}

\subsubsection{Public Engagement (digital
participation)}\label{public-engagement-digital-participation}

Again, from the right to the city, Mitchell (2003) highlights the right
to the city, which is \emph{oeuvre}\footnote{A piece of art {[}in
  French{]}}, a piece of art, that each citizen contributes to (Tedeschi
2024, 3). It is a piece of art, like a tapestry, with which city
dwellers can create by sharing their lived experiences and lead that to
actions (Tedeschi 2024, 6; Currie, Knox, and Mcgregor 2022; Martin
2025). Taking citizens' lived experiences and emotions towards a space
through the planning and design process would lead to a valued space, or
the so-called place (Martin 2025). Moreover, the public participation
has always better received with urban projects regardless of being
effective or not (Martin 2025).

Even re-framing the Right to the City leads to co-design from a
governmental perspective towards shaping public space rather than just
having citizens gain more power and rights over their space (Tedeschi
2024).

Taking the notions explained about the archival media, the autonomous
authorship would lead to having a democratized media (Haskins 2007;
Martin 2025). This can be recognized as the first steps towards public
participation before engaging the stakeholders, mostly citizens or
vulnerable groups. To understand the extent of participation, Arnstein
(2019) suggested the concept of ``The Ladder of Participation,'' which
indicates the level of public engagement and participation in the form
of a ladder structure with changing levels. The ladder starts with the
least level of participation known as Therapy till the most ultimate
form, which is active citizen engagement on design process.

Based on Arnstein (2019) `s ladder of participation, having the shared
lived experiences of citizens on digital platforms may be a lower step,
yet a passively engaging position that needs to be updated. Because this
is not about active interaction of the planner/designer with citizens,
but reading or tracing citizens' emotions on an autonomous semi-survey
publicly available (Martin 2025).

One positive aspect of having the digital inputs is the large scale of
data. Despite usually having a limited number of active participants in
urban projects, the digital platforms provide the opportunity to receive
qualitative inputs from a larger scope and cover more citizens sharing
their opinions and experiences (Martin 2025; Fathullah and Willis 2018).

The digital participation is also supported by different studies (Martin
2025; Haskins 2007). Also, Williamson and Ruming (2020) points out a
further step of using the social media data on a participatory scale.
They suggested the term `Frontloading' that refers to engaging the
stakeholders in the early stages of planning through accessing the
shared emotions and experiences on social media. (Read through
\#MySydney case)

\section{Conceptual Framework}\label{conceptual-framework}

The initial conceptual framework will help to extract the necessary
criteria to run the sentiment analysis on the collected data text
strings from the targeted platforms. The conceptual framework is based
on the principles driven out in the literature review, considering the
spatial qualities of how a space would picture memories and
lived-experiences of citizens when they interact with it. And, how this
can be identified and classified through semantic analysis of string
data on online platforms.

The initial concept would be the transformation of space to place
through people's interactions that shapes sense of belonging towards
their environment based on several spatial and contextual factors
(Martin 2025).

}

\end{figure}%

The belonging factors can be identified as below in Fig. 4, which
represents a very initial draft of spatial and contextual aspects that
would make a direct and indirect impact of ones sense of belonging
towards the environment in which they have been and interacted within
it.

\begin{figure}[H]

{\centering \pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{images/CamScanner 12-19-2024 19.48_9.jpg}}

}

affecting factors}}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  Ecology: green \& blue elements, living species
\item
  Accessibility: sidewalk, pathways, entrances, gateways
\item
  Openness: open space, tiling, profile details
\item
  Sitting space: edges, fountain, benches
\item
  Landmarks: City Theater building, statues, fountain
\item
  Visibility: Eye view sheds, landmarks, corridors,
\item
  Safety: pedestrian priority, traffic control
\item
  Security: active presence, observer eye (eyes on the street)
\item
  Geography: topography, location
\item
  Climate: temperature, humidity, sun angle
\item
  Identity: symbols, culture, language, lifestyle
\end{itemize}

The memories of space or place that are turned into lived experience and
expressed through emotions in digital space are useful assets to analyze
a space from a cognitive view.

The users share different experiences of their presence and interaction
within the lived space on social media. As mentioned before, their posts
and tweets would hold various emotions. The main gap would be how to
trace the spatial feature out of these shared emotions.

Thanks to the Lynch (1964)'s cognitive analysis concepts and methods of
the perceived space or city, the spatial features could be tracked and
recognized by how the individuals described their situation or position
of the space ad their mentions could be classified to Lynch's cognitive
features of nodes, lines, areas, edges and landmarks. The further
workflow would be focused on relating different sentiments to each
corresponding spatial feature of the context. For example, someone could
have gotten angry due to the presence in a specific area with edges.

The sentimental classification of each feature would help to come up
with valuable places and how they were designed, as a corresponding
design, and spaces that need improvement. This would help with further
steps of the design process and producing spatial conditions over spaces
that hold memories that can be recalled as a public good.

\section{Methodology (methods and
planning)}\label{methodology-methods-and-planning}

The main target is to collect the data from posts and tweets shared by
public profiles through social media platforms of Instagram and X
(formerly Twitter). The analysis frame will be concentrated on the
latest interventions happened in the City Theater Square. There are
pre-intervention and post-intervention phases in which the public shared
their opinions, expressions, and impressions as lived experiences when
the square was intact versus when it was transformed through re-design
procedures in the past two years of a time-stretched project (Aleksandar
et al., n.d.).

The main variable that will be collected is the text strings within
tweets on X and post captions on Instagram, using a scraping application
specialized for these platforms \footnote{Tweet scraper V2-X for Twitter
  and Instagram scraper} considering the legal and regulatory procedures
of online scraping. The data collection will follow a specified
time-frame mentioned before and defined keywords to narrow the search
domain as specifically as possible. Names like ``Teatré Shahr'',
``Teatré Shahr square'', or ``Vali'asr intersection'' would be used as
keywords to identify and collect any related text data to the chosen
case study(Williamson and Ruming 2020).

After that, the data needed to be cleaned of unnecessary details or
non-relevant expressions, so the analysis would be applied
appropriately. The unnecessary details it includes the personal
information and opinions on highly-sensitive topics that may cause risk
to data subjects. The necessary data would be related to sentiments,
spatial features, and memorial expressions that refer to the space
(i.e., City Theater and its surroundings), which are all texts that will
be collected in a string format. After that, the cleaned data will be
classified based on sentiment values driven from the structured
conceptual framework as qualitative content analysis(Williamson and
Ruming 2020). By overlapping the driven criteria with semantically
classified data, classified as various expressions of anger, sadness,
happiness, fear, etc., the spatial qualities and design guiding values
could be shaped or at least frame an overview of what is valuable and
should be preserved, and what might be the alternatives of design for
such public spaces.

\subsection{Expected Outcomes}\label{expected-outcomes}

The expected outcomes are considered to be either the results of data
analysis, spatial design guidelines, or interventions on the case study,
aka Teatré Shahr square.

\begin{figure}[H]

{\centering \pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{images/Expected Outcomes.png}}

}

\caption{\emph{Figure 8. Expected Outcomes}}

\end{figure}%

The outcomes would be categorized into results of the sentiment analysis
of text strings collected from tweets as an archive of emotions, along
with their linked spatial features that were matched together based on
cognitive indicators of urban space perception. This leads to having
valued spatial features based on emotions as negative or positive
elements within the space. These values will be localized based on their
in-text descriptions of the space in the form of hand drawings, map
drawings, and images generated via xFigura \footnote{An AI assistant
  platform meant for architectural design and presentation}.

On the spatial category, the outcomes are followed up on the analysis
outputs. The design guideline would be the first step in approaching the
challenges by creating concise and straightforward guidelines for the
public space that can also be used as a general design framework. After
that, some detailed interventions followed by the structured guideline
will be demonstrated using hand drawings, plans, sections, or bird-eye
view drawings of the integrated design work. These outcomes will be
presented as integration of manual drawings and AI-generated images,
such as xFigura.
